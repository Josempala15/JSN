Why litter matters

Litter doesn't stay where it's dropped. Rain and wind carry it into drains, streams and rivers, and eventually the sea. Animals can swallow it or get tangled in it, and plastics break into tiny fragments (microplastics) that spread through soil and water.

Clean verges, parks, riverbanks and beaches are better for wildlife and for people.

Litter and wildlife

  • 914 species are known to be affected by marine debris: 701 by eating it, 354 by entanglement.1
  • Among petrels, albatrosses and shearwaters, 41% of birds checked had plastic inside them, on average 9.9 pieces each.1
  • All 7 sea turtle species, 81 of 123 marine mammal species and 203 of 406 seabird species have been recorded eating or tangled in plastic.2
  • Another review found about 92% of cases involved plastic, 17% of the species were near threatened to critically endangered, and nearly 80% of entanglement cases caused harm or death.3

Most studies are of sea life; land animals are less studied.

1. Kühn & van Franeker (2020), Marine Pollution Bulletin – summary.
2. Kühn, Bravo Rebolledo & van Franeker (2015), in Marine Anthropogenic Litter, Springer – doi.
3. Gall & Thompson (2015), Marine Pollution Bulletin, Plymouth University – summary.

How long does litter last?

A ten-year field study followed cigarette butts (the most common litter item) in different places. Share of the butt's mass lost after 10 years:

Bare surface (like a street)~52%
Sandy soil66–76%
Grassland soilup to 84%

Even in the best case, part of the filter was still there after a decade, broken into microplastic fragments. Toxicity was highest just after smoking and peaked again mid-way through decay.

You'll often see "450 years" for plastic bottles. Those are estimates rather than measurements – a 2024 review of 38 studies said accurately measuring plastic breakdown over time is still a challenge.

Bonanomi et al. (2026), “Long-term cigarette butts' decomposition over 10 years…”, Environmental Pollution 397 – doi. Review: Aging of plastics and microplastics in the environment (Dec 2024).

What you can do

  • Carry a reusable bottle, cup and bag.
  • Take your rubbish home if a bin is full – never leave it beside it.
  • Check your council's recycling rules and rinse containers.
  • Choose less packaging and repair or reuse before buying new.
  • Report, photograph and map litter on JSN, then join a community clean.
  • Put cigarette ends in a bin, never the drain or ground.

Fast fashion

Clothes are litter too when they're binned, and cheap, short-lived fashion adds up.

  • The UK bought 1.42 million tonnes of textiles in 2022 and put about 711,000 tonnes of used textiles in general waste – around 35 items per person a year. Of the textiles in England's general waste, 84% was incinerated and 11% went to landfill.1
  • Making textiles produces about 1.2 billion tonnes of greenhouse gases a year, more than international flights and shipping combined.2
  • A UK parliamentary report noted synthetic fibres turning up in the deep sea, Arctic sea ice, fish and shellfish.3

What helps: buy less and choose lasting pieces, buy second-hand, repair and swap, give unwanted clothes to charity shops or textile banks instead of the bin, wash synthetics less often and cooler, and be wary of vague “eco” claims.

1. WRAP, Textiles Market Situation Report 2024 – press release.
2. Ellen MacArthur Foundation (2017), as reported by resource.co.
3. UK Environmental Audit Committee (2019), as reported by the World Economic Forum.

Litter-pick safely

  • Wear gloves and sturdy shoes; use a litter picker.
  • Never touch needles, broken glass or chemicals – report them to your council.
  • Stay off busy roads, steep banks and fast water.
  • Bring a bag for rubbish and one for recycling.
  • Fly-tipping is a crime – photograph it from a distance and report it.

Protect nature while you clean

  • Look before you pick – don't disturb nests, burrows or plants.
  • Keep to paths in fragile places like dunes, heath and wetlands.
  • Take only rubbish; leave shells, stones and plants where they are.
